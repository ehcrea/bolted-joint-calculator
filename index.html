Method, assumptions and references
  • The calculation follows VDI 2230 Part 1 (2015) for a single bolt with a concentric load (no bending, no prying). Eccentric loading, multi-bolt flanges and bending of the bolt (VDI 2230 Part 2) are not covered in this draft.
  • Bolt resilience δS is the sum of the head, shank, free thread, engaged thread and nut / tapped thread zones (lSK = 0.5d hex, 0.4d socket head; lG = 0.5d; lM = 0.4d nut, 0.33d tapped thread).
  • Plate resilience δP uses the deformation cone / sleeve substitute body with the cone angle φ from VDI 2230 eq. (5.1/42, 5.1/43). Parts of different materials are combined with a length-weighted series modulus.
  • Embedding fZ from VDI 2230 table 5 per thread, per head/nut bearing and per inner interface; transverse loads use the shear column. Inserts add one thread interface.
  • Thread stripping uses the shear areas of the ISO basic profile (FED-STD-H28 / ISO 898 form) with τB = fτ·Rm, the nut dilation factor C1 (VDI 2230) and a combined thread bending and tolerance factor 0.9. Heli-Coil: STI tapped hole major Ø ≈ d + 1.3P. Solid inserts: outer thread entered by the user; the steel insert body is assumed with Rm = 800 MPa.
  • Fatigue: endurance limit of the thread σASV = 0.85(150/d + 45) (rolled before heat treatment) or σASG = (2 − FSm/F0.2)·σASV (rolled after), for N ≥ 2·10⁶ cycles.
  • Limiting surface pressures pG are from VDI 2230 table A9 where available; values marked ~ in the material library are typical estimates. Always check with the supplier's data for your exact material and condition.

References: VDI 2230 Blatt 1:2015 Systematic calculation of highly stressed bolted joints; ISO 898-1, ISO 3506-1, ISO 261, ISO 724, ISO 273, ISO 4017, ISO 4032, ISO 4762, ISO 7089/7093, ISO 16047 (torque/clamp force testing), DIN 8140 (wire thread inserts); FED-STD-H28/2B (thread shear areas); Bickford, Introduction to the Design and Behavior of Bolted Joints.

Bolted Joint Calculator

Draft version 0.1 · by E. Tozlu

A free, browser-based tool for engineers, makers, designers and students to size and check bolted joints: preload, tightening torque, the joint diagram, the deformation cone of the clamped parts, embedding, temperature effects, fatigue, surface pressure under the head and thread stripping in nuts, tapped holes, Heli-Coil wire inserts and solid threaded inserts. All calculations run in your browser and every formula is shown with your numbers.

The method follows the public literature on bolted joints, mainly VDI 2230 Part 1, with friction data from VDI 2230 and ISO 16047 coating specifications. How to use the Bolted Joint Calculator

New here? Pick an example from Load application example… in the toolbar, then change one value at a time and watch the results.

Five steps

  1. Joint type: a through bolt with a nut, a bolt in a tapped hole, a bolt in a Heli-Coil (wire thread insert) or in a solid threaded insert.
  2. Bolt: thread size, property class, head type, plain shank and washers.
  3. Clamped parts: the stack of parts between head and nut (or tapped part), with material and thickness; the hole and the width of material around the bolt; for tapped joints the material and the thread engagement length.
  4. Friction & tightening: the surface and lubrication sets the friction range; the tightening method sets the preload scatter αA. Tighten to 90 % of the yield point (VDI default) or enter a torque.
  5. Loads per bolt: axial (static or cyclic), transverse, extra clamp load, temperature change.

Key terms

Preload FM
Tension in the bolt after tightening. It scatters between FM,min and FM,max = αA·FM,min because of friction and the tightening tool.
Stress area AS
Cross-section used for the bolt stress, with the mean of the pitch and minor diameters.
Load factor Φ
Share of the axial load that adds to the bolt force. The rest only reduces the clamp load. A stiff joint and an elastic bolt give a small Φ.
Deformation cone
The zone of the clamped parts that carries the clamp load, spreading from the head at the angle φ.
Embedding FZ
Preload lost when the surface roughness peaks flatten after assembly.
Residual clamp load FKR
Clamp force left in the interface under the maximum load: it must stay above what is needed for friction grip or sealing.
Thread stripping
The thread flanks shear off. Good design makes the bolt break first: a longer engagement or an insert in soft materials.

The Calculation steps section shows every formula with your numbers so you can follow or check the result by hand.

Galling

Stainless steel and titanium are protected by a thin oxide layer and harden quickly. Under thread pressure and sliding, the oxide is wiped off and the surfaces cold-weld: the torque jumps, the joint seizes before the preload is reached, and the bolt can twist off or the thread tear out. This is galling (thread seizure).

  • Highest risk: a stainless or titanium bolt in a mating thread of a like material (a stainless nut, a Heli-Coil, which is stainless wire, or a tapped hole in stainless or titanium), with dry threads. The tool warns for this pair, for a stainless or titanium bolt with dry threads in general, and when a friction option for steel bolts is chosen for a stainless or titanium bolt (those values are wrong for it too).
  • Notes only: a wet thread-locker (it lubricates only partly) and custom friction values with a like-material pair. With an anti-seize paste (stainless or titanium option, or MoS2) there is no message.
  • Prevention: anti-seize paste on the thread and under the head; coated or silver-plated bolts; materials of different hardness (e.g. a Nitronic 60 or coated steel nut, dry-film-coated Heli-Coils); coarse threads rather than fine; slow, steady tightening (no impact or fast power drivers, which heat the thread); clean threads; do not reuse galled parts.

Galling is not a calculated limit: it depends on surface finish, speed, cleanliness and coating, so the tool gives warnings and notes rather than a pass/fail check. The dry titanium friction range is an estimate.

Bolt patterns

Tick Loads act on a bolt pattern in section 6 and enter the forces and moments on the whole flange. The axial load Fz can act off-centre at (xF, yF). The bolts form a circle, a rectangular grid, or custom positions: paste x, y in mm, one bolt per line, from any origin (a drawing datum, or two columns from a spreadsheet). For custom patterns the coordinates and xF, yF use your origin; the tool moves everything to the bolt-group centroid C, which is shown in the plan view. Start from copies the current circle or grid into the list so you can edit single bolts. By default the tool treats the flange as rigid (the classical bolt-group model):

  • Shear Fx, Fy is shared equally; torsion Mz adds a shear Mz·ri/Σr² at right angles to each bolt radius.
  • Tension Fz is shared equally; bending adds M·yi/Σy² about the bolt-group centroid (centroid model, valid while the preloaded joint stays closed; for unsymmetric patterns with Σxy ≠ 0 the tool solves Σx²·b + Σxy·c = −My, Σxy·b + Σy²·c = Mx and uses FA,i = Fz/n + b·xi + c·yi; bolts on one line cannot carry the moment about that line, which is flagged) or M·hi/Σh² about the flange edge (heel model, for a joint that has opened or for ultimate loads; for custom patterns the edge lies the given distance beyond the outermost bolt).
  • The bolt with the highest tension and the highest shear are combined into one design bolt (conservative if they are different bolts), which runs through all the checks.

Flexible flange (rectangular patterns, centroid model). A rigid flange shares a load by lever arms alone. A real flange bends, so the bolts close to a local load take more and the distant ones less. Tick Flexible flange to model the flange as a beam along x (Euler–Bernoulli, width Ly, thickness t, E of the first clamped part) resting on one spring per bolt column:

  • Spring stiffness per bolt k = 1/δS + 1/δP, the stiffness of the closed, preloaded joint against an external load (bolt stretching and parts unloading in parallel). It comes from the same VDI 2230 resiliences as the single-bolt check.
  • Fz at xF and My load the beam. It is solved with beam finite elements (exact for point loads), giving each column load R = kcol·w, the deflection line and the largest flange bending moment. Along y the flange stays rigid: Mx (including Fz·yF) is shared by yi/Σy².
  • The result is compared with the rigid model bolt by bolt, and the flange bending stress Mmax/(Ly·t²/6) is checked against Rp. A very thick flange gives the rigid result; a thin one puts most of the load on the nearest bolt.

The flexible model assumes a rigid base, a joint that stays closed at every bolt, and a flange that acts as a beam (long compared with its width). Plate effects across the width, a flexible base and opened joints need FEA. Prying forces and the VDI 2230 eccentric case of a single bolt are separate options (sections 5 and 6). Circular flanges loaded through a pipe or hub are symmetric enough that the rigid model fits well.

Prying

When a web or an angle leg pulls on a flange, the flange bends, its outer edge presses on the support, and it levers an extra force Q onto the bolt: the bolt carries B = T + Q. Tick Flange prying in section 5 and enter, per bolt, the distance to the web face b, the distance to the flange edge a, the flange width per bolt w and the flange thickness t.

  • Prying ratio (Douty & McGuire elastic formula, in Kulak, Fisher & Struik): Q/T = (½ − X/30) / ((a/b)·(a/3b + 1) + X/6) with X = w·t⁴/(a·b²·Ab). A thick flange (X above 15) does not pry; a is limited to 1.25·b.
  • The prying force is added to the axial load of the bolt (as in ECSS-E-HB-32-23A), so all checks, including fatigue and NASA-STD-5020, see it. With a bolt pattern it acts on the most loaded bolt.
  • Flange bending is checked at the web face (|T·b − Q·a|) and at the bolt line (Q·a, net width w − dh) against the yield strength of the first clamped part.

The formula is elastic and assumes a flange that does not yield. Steel construction codes (e.g. AISC, EN 1993-1-8 T-stub) use plastic models with other limits. Reduce prying with a thicker flange, bolts closer to the web, or stiffeners.

Eccentric clamping and loading

Many joints are not symmetric: the bolt sits off the centre of the contact face (eccentric clamping, offset ssym), or the load acts beside the bolt (eccentric loading, offset a), for example on a bracket or a cover flange. The parts then bend as well as compress. Tick Eccentric clamping and loading in section 5 and enter the size of the contact face cT × bT and the offsets, measured from the centre of the contact face, positive towards the edge U where it would open first.

  • Load factor: the bending resilience of the parts βP = lK/(EP·ĪBers) gives δP* = δP + ssym²·βP and δP** = δP + ssym·a·βP, and Φen* = n·δP**/(δS + δP*). A load further out than the bolt (a > ssym) raises the load factor, often several times.
  • One-sided opening: the joint lifts at U unless the residual clamp load is at least FKA = (FA·(a − ssym)·u + MB·u)/(IBT/AD + ssym·u). FKA enters the required clamp load and has its own check in R10.
  • Bolt bending: the bending of the parts also bends the bolt. The alternating stress in R9 is multiplied by 1 + (a/Φen* − ssym)·(lK/lers)·(ES/EP)·π·dS³/(8·ĪBers).
  • ĪBers, the bending inertia of the clamped parts, is estimated from a cylinder with the same axial resilience as δP, limited to IBT of the contact face. This tends to be low, so bolt bending comes out on the safe side; enter a better value (FEA, or the deformation-body formulas of VDI 2230 Part 1) when you have one.

Valid only while the joint stays closed. Once it opens on one side, the bolt load rises steeply and non-linearly (VDI 2230 treats this separately); redesign so that it stays closed. The formulas follow VDI 2230 Part 1 (2015) in simplified form and should be checked against the guideline for critical joints. Do not combine with prying: both describe the same bending flange.

Aerospace check (NASA-STD-5020)

Spaceflight hardware for NASA follows NASA-STD-5020 (threaded fastening systems); ESA uses ECSS-E-HB-32-23A. Tick Also check to NASA-STD-5020 in section 6 to run it next to VDI 2230 on the same joint. The main differences:

  • Preload range from the torque range: Ppi = (1 ± Γ)·T / (K·d), with the nut factor K at the mean friction, the drawing torque tolerance, and the preload uncertainty Γ (by default ±25 % lubricated, ±35 % dry; smaller from torque–tension tests or measured preload). The running torque of a locking feature is subtracted unless the torque is specified above it.
  • Service preload: minus relaxation (the larger of a percentage and the VDI 2230 embedding) and temperature effects.
  • Factored limit loads with factors of safety and a fitting factor, and margins of safety MS = allowable / factored load − 1, which must be ≥ 0: separation and slip with the minimum preload, yield with the maximum preload, ultimate tension without preload (ductile fastener), thread shear-out, bolt shear and the tension–shear interaction.
  • Locking feature required on safety-critical fasteners.

This is a simplified implementation for design studies: the defaults are typical values, and your programme's revision of the standard, its factors of safety and its allowables (e.g. MMPDS) govern. The load factor nφ comes from the VDI 2230 stiffness model.

Locking elements

A joint comes loose in two ways: it loses preload (settling, creep, temperature), or the screw turns loose under transverse vibration (Junker test, DIN 65151 / ISO 16130). Enough preload is the first defence against both. Locking elements help with only one of them:

  • Split lock washer (DIN 127, withdrawn in 2003): pressed flat far below the preload, then only adds contact faces that settle. VDI 2230 Part 1 calls it ineffective in preloaded joints. The calculator adds its layer and the extra embedding, and warns.
  • Conical spring washer (DIN 6796) or disc springs: a spring in series with the bolt. Once settling lowers the force below its flat load it springs back and keeps most of the preload. Calculated with a linearised spring rate from the flat load and the spring travel.
  • Wedge-locking washer pair (tested to DIN 25201): stops the screw turning loose, but does not stop settling. Calculated as a washer pair with one more contact face.
  • Thread-locking adhesive (anaerobic, ISO 10964; MIL-S-22473 / ASTM D5363 in defence): stops the screw turning loose and seals the thread. Applied wet, it changes the friction during tightening, so choose a thread-locker friction entry. It adds no preload and does not stop settling.

Lock washer dimensions, flat loads and thread-locker friction are typical catalogue values (marked ~ or noted as estimates): check your supplier's data.

Mounting circuit boards (PCBs)

Choose PCB FR-4 as a clamped part. A board behaves differently from a metal plate:

  • Soft through its thickness. FR-4 has Ez ≈ 10 GPa, about 1/20 of steel. Under preload the board compresses 15 µm, more than the screw stretches (8 µm), so a large share of any pull-off load (ΦK ≈ 0.6 for an M3 through 1.6 mm) reaches the screw.
  • That softness helps against settling. The embedding of head, washer and thread (about 9 µm) costs 58 % of the minimum preload, against all of it in a 1.6 mm steel plate. Thicker boards keep more preload.
  • The board limits the torque, not the screw. At 0.6 N·m, the highest torque that keeps the board pressure within 2/3 of its limit, an M3 8.8 screw uses only about 45 % of its yield strength.
  • Creep is the main long-term loss, especially above about 80 °C and near the glass transition Tg. The calculator warns about it but does not compute it.
  • Temperature: FR-4 expands about 60 ppm/K through its thickness (about 250 ppm/K above Tg), about 5 times as much as a steel screw. Heating raises the screw force and the board pressure; cooling lowers the clamp.

Good practice:

  • Clamp on a bare copper or ENIG pad (a ring free of solder mask) a little larger than the washer.
  • Always use a washer, or a screw with a built-in washer or flange. Without one the head presses about 115 MPa into the board at 0.6 N·m (limit 100 MPa).
  • Use about 0.5–0.6 N·m for M3 with a controlled screwdriver. The lower end keeps at least 25 % of the preload after settling, the upper end keeps the board pressure within 2/3 of its limit. Against vibration use thread-locking compound or a locking feature rather than more torque.
  • Never tap threads into the board: use a nut, a standoff, or a press-fit or SMD nut.
  • Keep ceramic capacitors (MLCCs), BGAs and other strain-sensitive parts a few millimetres away from mounting holes.

The FR-4 values (Ez, expansion and the 100 MPa pressure limit) are estimates: no standard gives a limiting pressure for circuit boards. Use your laminate supplier's data where it matters.
